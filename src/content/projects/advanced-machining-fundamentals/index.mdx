This teaching project was developed to address a recurring gap in machining education: students could follow feeds-and-speeds tables but lacked a generalized understanding of why machines behave the way they do. I designed and delivered a multi-part lecture series that reframes machining from first principles, starting with material hardness (BHN), unit power, spindle torque, and duty cycles.

Rather than optimizing for specific tools or machines, the framework emphasizes transferable reasoning applicable across different CNC platforms and materials. Topics include spindle power curves, thermal limits, tool engagement dynamics, chip thinning, and the tradeoffs between high-speed and high-efficiency machining.

The goal was to help students replace fear-driven conservatism with bounded, explainable process tuning. The deck is a public record of the curriculum; the reported influence on shop discussions and later workshops is practical teaching context rather than a measured educational study.

## Series Design Goal
**Insight:** Replace memorized feeds-and-speeds with portable first-principles reasoning

The lecture sequence was built to help students estimate safe and efficient cuts from machine limits, material properties, and engagement geometry. Instead of treating CAM presets as fixed truths, each module teaches how to derive a defensible starting point and adjust from measured behavior at the machine.

## Core Technical Modules
**Insight:** Power, torque, chip-load physics, and thermal limits tied into one decision flow

The middle lectures connect spindle power curves, torque at low RPM, radial chip thinning, and heat generation so students can predict when a strategy will chatter, rub, or overload. This made it easier to choose between high-efficiency and high-speed paths based on machine capability rather than preference.

## Operational Impact in the Shop
**Insight:** Machine feedback closes the loop between a calculated starting point and a reliable process

In workshops, students used simple calculations and machine constraints to justify parameter changes, then compared those expectations with spindle load, sound, chip formation, surface finish, and tool condition. The material was later reused in SOP discussions and advanced sessions. Those observations support the curriculum's practical use, but they do not substitute for a controlled assessment of learning outcomes.

## A Reusable Decision Sequence
**Insight:** Separate instantaneous cutting demand from the machine's time-dependent thermal capacity

A representative calculation moves from material and tool limits to surface speed, spindle speed, chip load, feed, engagement, and estimated power. Two relationships keep the chain visible:

$$
v_f = n z f_z
$$

and

$$
P = \tau \omega
$$

The resulting cutting-power demand should be compared with the spindle's speed-dependent power and torque curves. Duty cycle is then treated as a duration and thermal-capacity constraint; it does not automatically reduce the instantaneous power required by the cut. This distinction is important when translating a short, aggressive toolpath into a safe continuous process.
